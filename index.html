Case study · Audience research

Selling what guests already love

Kona Village markets its iconic hale bungalows, but our review analysis showed guests talk about the property no more than they do at Four Seasons next door. What they rave about is the food and the service. I helped identify that gap and the competitor behind it, then wrote the interview guide and designed the survey to test whether leading with dining and service would win over first-time guests.

Brand
Kona Village, A Rosewood Resort
Where
USC Annenberg, DSM 550 Analytics and Research Methodology
Team
5 people · Fall 2026
Status
In progress

01The challenge

Kona Village reopened under Rosewood in 2023 after more than a decade closed, so most luxury travelers have never stayed there. Its growth depends on first-time guests, and right next door is Four Seasons Hualālai, at a similar price, with a similar setting and a similar cultural story. When two resorts look this alike on paper, a first-timer decides based on what the marketing and other guests say. We set out to find what actually makes Kona Village different, in guests' own words.

02My role

I helped pin down Four Seasons Hualālai as Kona Village's real competitor and identify where Kona actually wins. I wrote the qualitative interview guide on my own, from the warm-up questions to the blind stimulus tests and the closing ranking exercise, and designed our quantitative survey. I also built the recruitment plan with my team, recruited participants through my network and LinkedIn, and wrote the consent form. I was part of every stage of the research process.

03The approach

We started by coding 100 Expedia reviews for each resort, and the gap jumped out. Kona Village sells its standalone hales and setting, yet guests mention the property at almost exactly the same rate as at Four Seasons (57% vs. 58%). Where Kona pulls ahead is in things the brand barely talks about: dining (32% vs. 19%) and service (52% vs. 46%). That turned into our central question: should Kona Village lead with the experiences guests already love?

To answer it, we designed a two-phase study. First come 30-minute blind interviews, where the resorts are only ever called Resort A and Resort B, so people react to the experience rather than the brand name. Participants compare unbranded Instagram-style photos and real review excerpts, then pick between two ad concepts, one built around the property and one around food and service, with one question at the center: which would make you stop scrolling? What we learn there shapes the survey, which screens for high-income travelers who have never stayed at Kona Village, ranks seven resort attributes, and tests four marketing messages in randomized order, measuring how likely people are to consider the resort before and after they see them.

04Where it stands

The research plan and instruments are complete and presented, and recruitment for interviews and the Qualtrics survey is underway. The final deliverables are an insights deck, a survey dataset, and a strategic report recommending what Kona Village should lead with in its marketing. I'll update this page with the findings.

Case study · Brand revival concept

Burberry, Reconnected

Burberry's sales were sliding, its image felt dated to younger shoppers, and outlets had grown to drive most of its revenue. I pitched the angle that shaped our whole project: new creative director Daniel Lee, who had made Bottega Veneta feel young again, was Burberry's way back to the next generation.

Where
Emerson College, Online Content & Strategy
Team
5 people · senior final
My part
Strategic angle, 6 of 10 pages, infographic
Audience
18–30, lapsed fans, Bottega fans

01The challenge

Burberry had a heritage problem. Sales had been falling for years, younger shoppers saw it as their parents' label, and outlets had grown to account for more than half of total sales, a sign the brand was being sold on discount rather than desire. Our brief: pick a struggling brand and build a fully functional website that could help bring it back to life.

02My role

I proposed Burberry and the strategic angle behind the whole project. The opening was Daniel Lee, the new creative director who had turned Bottega Veneta into a label young people wanted, with breakout signature pieces and its now-famous green. If he could do it there, Burberry had a real shot at winning back a younger audience. On the build, I co-created the Wix site with one teammate, designing six of its ten pages (Home, About, Our Vision, Sustainability, Collection, and the Campaign page), and created the campaign infographic.

03The approach

We aimed at 18–30-year-olds, plus two audiences we didn't want to leave on the table: people who used to love Burberry, and Bottega fans who had followed Daniel Lee. Our insight was that this generation respects heritage when it feels personal, not when it's put behind glass. So the campaign, Rewritten Heritage, treats the trench and the check as raw material for self-expression, built on creator collaborations, immersive drops centered on craft and sustainability, and short-form, social-first storytelling.

My pages carry the core of that story: from Burberry's heritage, to the vision for its future, to sustainability, the collection, and finally the campaign itself, so a visitor understands why the brand matters before we ask them to see it differently. The infographic, How Burberry Can Win Back Gen Z, put the whole argument on one page: why the brand was losing younger shoppers to rivals like Gucci, Prada, and Off-White, what research says Gen Z actually wants, and four moves to win them back: streetwear and cultural collaborations, social-first storytelling, accessible entry-level and unisex products, and leading with sustainability.

04The outcome

A live, fully built brand site with an original podcast, embedded video, a press kit, a blog, and a sign-up form, presented to the class as a 20–30 minute pitch. More than anything, it taught me to start with the business problem and let every piece of content answer it.

Case study · Content creation

A small account, run like a lab

Since March 2025, I've run a Rednote account about Diudiu, my mini chocolate dachshund, doing every part of it myself. It hasn't had a breakout yet, and that's what makes it useful: every post is a small test of who's watching, when, and why something earns a save.

Platform
Rednote (Xiaohongshu)
Star
Diudiu, mini chocolate dachshund
Since
March 2025 · nearly 900 likes & saves
My part
Everything: concept, shoot, edit, post

01The positioning

The account is cozy, healing everyday life with Diudiu: the kind of content people save at the end of a long day. Its voice is his, from the bio onward, so the account reads like a small dog introducing his world.

02What I've learned works

Timing follows the audience. Different niches reach different people who scroll at different hours. For pet content, I've found the best windows are around 9 AM and between 6 and 8 PM.

Let the pet tell the story. Videos from the pet's first-person point of view, and audio built as a conversation between pet and owner, tend to travel further.

Pets meet luxury. Young owners treat their pets like kids and want the best for them, and pet goods are one of the most affordable ways into a luxury brand. My "LouisV puppy" post played on exactly that idea.

03The results

Across the account, my posts have earned nearly 900 likes and saves. The LouisV post is my most-viewed so far, with 5,236 views, more than 70 times my follower count, so it clearly reached well beyond the people already following. Several new pet food and supply brands have reached out about sponsored posts. I turned them down, because I couldn't vouch for products I didn't know to the people who trust the account.

04What it taught me

Running my own account changed how I look at content. I stopped asking only whether something looks good, and started asking why it gets attention: who it's for, when they're watching, and what makes them stop.

About

When I build a campaign, I'm thinking about how it'll be made. When I make content, I'm thinking about who it's for.

I'm Avery Liu. I started behind the camera, studying film production at Emerson College, and I most recently worked as an Assistant Editor at Boston Production Inc, cutting complete videos from scripts and notes. Somewhere between the set and the edit bay, I got just as curious about the audience as about the story. By senior year, I was building a brand revival campaign for Burberry alongside my film work.

Now I'm pursuing a master's in Digital Social Media at USC Annenberg, where I've been working on research for Kona Village (Rosewood) to sharpen how a luxury resort markets itself to first-time guests. I've also been running my own pet-focused Rednote account since March 2025, where I write, shoot, edit, and post every piece myself.

That mix is my edge. I want to bring both halves to entertainment marketing.

Off camera, I play cello and tinker with sound engineering, which is probably why I always notice the audio first.
